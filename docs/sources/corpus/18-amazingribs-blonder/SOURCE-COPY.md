## Article 1 — "What Causes The BBQ Stall: It's Not What You Think"

**URL:** `https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/understanding-and-beating-barbecue-stall/`
**Author:** Meathead Goldwyn (AmazingRibs.com founder, BBQ Hall of Fame), reporting Prof. Greg Blonder's
research. Blonder: physicist/engineer, former Chief Technical Advisor at Bell Labs, professor at Boston
University (BS physics MIT, PhD Harvard per source map).
**Last modified (per page):** July 20, 2026 (article is continuously maintained; original research dates
to Blonder's 2016 work per the source map).

**Core finding:** the BBQ "stall" is **evaporative cooling**, not collagen melting or fat rendering.

**Experimental data (bowl-of-water-in-oven experiments, varying oven set temperature):**
- 125°F oven → water stalled at ≈115°F
- 175°F oven → water stalled at ≈140°F
- 225°F oven → water stalled at ≈160°F
- 425°F+ oven → stall approaches 212°F (boiling point) — i.e. the effect nearly vanishes at high heat

**Meat vs. fat vs. wet-sponge control:**
- Pure beef fat: steady rise, no stall (rules out fat rendering as the cause)
- Wet cellulose sponge: stalled at ≈140°F for 4+ hours, mimicking a real brisket stall, then resumed
  heating once dried — direct evidence for evaporative cooling as the mechanism

**Pork shoulder trial (6 lb butt):** foil-wrapped piece reached 180°F in ≈6 hours; unwrapped piece was
still under 180°F after a comparable time and was noted as "slightly tough." Wrapping (the "Texas crutch")
short-circuits the stall by blocking evaporation.

**Airflow relationship:** "The more airflow there is in the oven, the lower the stall temp." Pellet
smokers with fans → shorter stall; low-airflow electric smokers → minimal/no stall.

**Practical figures:** stall onset typically 150–170°F internal, after roughly 2–3 hours of cooking at
225°F; can last several hours (6+ observed). Target internal temp for "probe tender" brisket referenced as
**≈203°F**.

## Article 2 — "What You Need to Know About Wood, Smoke, And Combustion"

**URL:** `https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/grill-and-smoker-setup-and-firing/science-of-wood-and-smoke/`
**Author:** Meathead Goldwyn. **Last modified:** July 22, 2026.

**Wood type table** (smoke intensity / heat output / spark / ember quality) → see `wood-type-table.csv`.

**Four combustion stages:**
1. Dehydration (up to ≈500°F) — water evaporates as steam
2. Decomposition/pyrolysis (500–700°F) — volatile organic compounds boil off
3. Combustion (700–1000°F) — gases ignite; guaiacol/syringol (desirable flavor compounds) form best in a
   650–750°F "sweet spot"; acrid compounds form above 750°F
4. Charcoal formation (above 1000°F) — pure carbon remains, minimal further smoke/flavor

**Wood composition:** cellulose ≈40%, hemicelluloses ≈40%, lignin ≈19%, minerals ≈1%. Lignin is stated as
the source of essentially all smoke flavor (cellulose contributes none). Full combustion yields ≈8,600
BTU/lb.

**Charcoal vs wood:** charcoal is pre-burned wood (near-pure carbon), burns hotter, produces fewer
combustion byproducts, and needs no flame management, vs raw wood.

**Form guidance:** chunks (golf-ball to fist size) for long cooks (225°F+) — most desirable for steady
release; logs need skill and are typically pre-burned to embers, need 275°F+ for clean smoke. Chips/pellets
for short cooks — pellets ≈20 min burn time at 225°F.

**Numeric parameters:** optimal aromatic-compound combustion range 650–750°F; recommended cooking temps
225°F (most meats) / 325°F (poultry); moisture targets: air-dried wood 25–30%, kiln-dried 15–22%; starting
wood amount ≈2 oz, up to 8 oz max for pork/beef, 4 oz for poultry; smoke penetration into meat rarely
exceeds 1/8 inch; "blue smoke" (optimal) particle size <1 micron. Nitric oxide (NO) formation (requires
sufficient oxygen) is stated as essential to smoke-ring formation.

**Explicit editorial claim (relevant to source-map gap G-C):** "smoke flavor is influenced more by climate
and soil and oxygen availability than wood species" and "controlling combustion is by far the most
important aspect of smoke flavor, far more important than the species of wood." This directly supports the
source map's conclusion that wood-species-to-meat pairing has **no authoritative backing anywhere** — even
this, the best available source, treats species choice as secondary to combustion control.

## Article 3 (bonus, not required by #18 but same publisher, relevant to disagreement D-7) — "Resting Meat: Why I Don't Believe The Hype"

**URL:** `https://amazingribs.com/more-technique-and-science/more-cooking-science/science-of-resting-meat/`
**Author:** Meathead Goldwyn. **Last modified:** July 21, 2026.

**Quantitative data cited (from Blonder's and Kenji López-Alt's own measurements):**
- Prof. Blonder, ribeye: unrested juice loss ≈1 oz (7%); rested 30 min ≈0.85 oz (≈15% less loss) — but
  carryover cooking raised the rested steak's center temp from 125°F to 145°F.
- Kenji López-Alt: unrested steak weight loss 6% greater than a 5-minute rest; difference <2.5 tbsp across
  a ≈17.6 oz steak.
- Prof. Blonder, pork loin: 3-min rest ≈3 oz juice loss vs 20-min rest ≈2 oz loss (1 oz difference on a
  33 oz roast); ≈0.5 oz of that evaporated during the rest itself rather than being "saved."

**Argument:** juiciness is not primarily a function of retained water but of softened/melted fat; resting
cools the crust (texture loss) and risks carryover overcooking; the measured juice differences are small
relative to total weight and much of the "saved" liquid isn't lost anyway (it lands on the board/plate and
is eaten with the meat, or is offset by evaporation).

This is presented in the source map (§7, §D-7) as a genuine **within-craft disagreement**, not something a
regulator adjudicates: FSIS's "3 minutes rest" rule is about pathogen lethality carryover, not moisture —
Blonder/Meathead's dispute is entirely about texture/juiciness and does not touch the FSIS 3-minute
requirement.
